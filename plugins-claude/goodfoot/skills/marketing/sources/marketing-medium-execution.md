# Executing in Your Medium

Part of the [Technology Marketing](./index.md) guide. Use this page after the
medium is chosen: it tells you how to produce well *within* video, imagery,
text, audio, or an interactive demo, and how to reinforce the message in a
second medium. Rules are ranked by measured effect size where the evidence
allows — fix the top of each list first. Directives extrapolated from
adjacent research (instructional design, industry data) carry an inline
confidence cue; lean on those proportionally.

The stage and audience decisions come first — see
[Marketing by Funnel Stage](./marketing-funnel-stages.md) and
[Marketing by Audience Segment](./marketing-audience-segments.md). No medium
rescues a message aimed at the wrong stage or stakeholder.

## Video and animation

Spend video on what it is uniquely good at: making a claim *believable* by
showing the real thing working. Video's persuasive advantage over
well-written text is small — the argument must still be in the script;
footage that merely decorates a claim adds nothing a paragraph wouldn't
(Wittenberg et al. 2021, PNAS).

Production rules, ranked by effect size (Cromley & Chen 2025 meta-analysis of
the multimedia-learning literature; instructional-research base, transfers
directly to demo and explainer production):

1. **Cut every decorative element** — stock footage, trivia, jokes, mood
   B-roll that doesn't carry the claim. Two meta-analyses agree decorative
   "seductive detail" actively hurts retention, it doesn't just fail to help
   (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020; Rey 2012) — the most reliable finding in
   this list.
2. **Narrate over graphics; don't put the narration's text on screen**
   (modality, d ≈ 0.72 overall and ≈ 0.93 for system-paced material;
   Ginns 2005). Full-text slides read aloud are the worst of both channels
   (redundancy; Mayer).
3. **Write narration in second person, conversationally** — "you push, it
   checks" beats "the system performs validation" (personalization,
   d ≈ 0.30 retention / 0.54 transfer; Ginns, Martin & Marsh 2013).
4. **Cue the eye to where the claim is being demonstrated** — arrows,
   highlights, motion emphasis (signaling, d ≈ 0.38; Alpizar, Adesope &
   Wong 2020; Richter, Scheiter & Eitel 2016).
5. **Chapter long videos and let the viewer control pace** (segmenting;
   small-to-medium effect, Rey et al. 2019 — do it, but after the items
   above).
6. **Use high-quality TTS freely.** The historical "human voice beats
   machine narration" advantage has closed with modern synthetic voices
   (Dinçer 2022); the bar is naturalness, not humanity.

## Imagery and static visuals

- **Use only images that carry information** — screenshots, diagrams,
  annotated diffs, architecture maps. An informative image out-recalls the
  equivalent text (picture superiority; dual-coding — Paivio; Defeyter et
  al. 2009): lead with it for anything the audience must remember.
- **Delete decorative stock photography.** The seductive-details effect
  applies to static imagery too: ornament draws fixations away from the
  substance and hurts retention (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020 —
  instructional-research base, same cognitive mechanism).
- **Maximize fluency: clean layout, high contrast, legible type, no
  clutter.** Fluently processed material is judged *truer* — low-fluency
  design measurably depresses the believability of the claims next to it
  (Reber & Schwarz 1999; Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman 2004).
- **Treat polish as a first-impression budget, not a credibility budget.**
  Visual-appeal judgments form in ~50 ms and stick (Lindgaard et al. 2006),
  so the thumbnail, hero image, and first frame deserve real design effort —
  but polish does not survive contact with a bad product experience (Tuch et
  al. 2012), and once someone evaluates an actual claim, verifiability and
  evident expertise matter as much as looks (Fogg et al. 2003). Budget
  accordingly: enough polish to be fluent and clean; the rest into provenance,
  real outputs, and named references.

## Text

- **Front-load: conclusion in the first two lines, key terms at the left
  edge of each block.** Readers scan in an F-shaped pattern — they read the
  beginning and skim the rest — so structure for scanning instead of fighting
  it (Nielsen Norman Group eye-tracking, replicated across devices). "People
  don't read" is false; "people won't dig for your point" is the actionable
  truth.
- Everything else about text is the core guide:
  [Marketing Message Checklist](./marketing-message-checklist.md) for the
  checks, [Marketing by Funnel Stage](./marketing-funnel-stages.md) for what
  the text should claim at each stage.

## Audio

*Confidence cue: directives here are extrapolated from instructional
narration research and industry data — solid direction, softer numbers.*

- **Write for a warm, direct, second-person register.** The personalization
  effect (d ≈ 0.30–0.54) is the strongest transferable finding for spoken
  content.
- **Prefer host-read-style delivery over produced spots** where the format
  allows: familiar-voice delivery outperforms standardized spots on recall
  and response in industry studies (Nielsen, Edison Research — advertiser-
  funded, not peer-reviewed; treat specific percentages as directional).
- **Synthetic voices are viable** (Dinçer 2022) — spend the savings on the
  script.

## Interactive demos

- **Make interaction pause the flow.** Interaction that halts playback while
  the user acts improves outcomes (g ≈ 0.52); interaction layered over
  continuous playback splits attention and can perform *worse* than passive
  video (Ploetzner 2022).
- **Optimize perceived responsiveness, not widget count.** *Felt*
  interactivity predicts engagement; the number of interactive features does
  not (Yang & Shen 2018).
- **Get the user's own material in fast.** Hands-on direct experience builds
  attitudes that are stronger, more confident, and far more predictive of
  later behavior than passive exposure (Fazio & Zanna 1978, 1981). This is
  why the demo — not the video — is the right asset for the trust and trial
  stages of the funnel.
- *Confidence cue:* no marketing-specific head-to-head of interactive demo
  vs. passive video exists; the directives above rest on educational and
  attitude research. Don't claim "demos convert better" — build the demo for
  attitude durability and measure conversion yourself.

## Reinforcing in a second medium

Reinforcement across media is measurably better than more repetition inside
one medium — done right:

- **Re-execute, don't re-skin.** Keep the core claim constant but make the
  second-medium creative visibly different. High creative "fit" between
  paired ads improved evaluation but *hurt* brand and campaign memory;
  varied executions encode more distinctly (Voorveld program, "The Fit
  Factor," Journal of Advertising).
- **Cross-media repetition beats same-medium repetition at equal
  frequency** on credibility, attitude, and intent (Chang & Thorson 2004;
  Voorveld et al.).
- **Budget the second medium generously.** Synergy is econometrically real,
  and as it rises the *weaker* medium's marginal contribution is undervalued
  by single-medium models — spend more there than instinct suggests (Naik &
  Raman 2003).
- **Cap frequency per audience member before wear-out.** Exposure–liking is
  an inverted U (Bornstein 1989; Schmidt & Eisend 2015): when reach starts
  repeating, vary the execution instead of adding exposures.
